Most cybersecurity products are designed for the people operating the tools. The CISO, despite being accountable for the organization’s entire security program, often receives information indirectly—summarized by teams, separated from the work, and already out of date by the time it reaches them.

Onyxia proposed a data-management layer built specifically for the CISO. Through integrations with security products, security BI, Jira, ServiceNow, and other operational systems, it brought the organization’s security activity into a single model the CISO could own.

The idea was to connect executive decisions with the work that followed them. A CISO could define benchmarks and SLAs, then see whether teams were actually meeting them. Instead of relying on a high-level status report, they could trace a decision down to an individual task, its owner, its progress, and the reason it was delayed.

That changed both daily operations and board-level conversations. In a team meeting, the CISO could pinpoint which tasks required attention rather than asking for another update. At the board, they could show where the previous year’s security budget had gone, what it had achieved, which threats remained, and where the next investment should be directed.

For me, the CISO was a new design persona. I had to understand what makes someone responsible for the entire security organization effective: how much detail they need, when they need to move from a benchmark into the underlying work, and how the same data must support both an operational meeting and a strategic budget discussion.

I joined as Product Design Team Lead, working with one additional designer based in the United States. Our mandate was to help Onyxia take the next step—from a strong product idea to a mature security platform with a coherent experience and visual language.

The engagement was short. The product required more fundamental change than the available resources allowed us to make, and I eventually decided to move on. But the central idea stayed with me: executive visibility shouldn’t mean receiving a better presentation. It should mean owning the data well enough to connect decisions, execution, and outcomes—and no longer operate in the dark.
