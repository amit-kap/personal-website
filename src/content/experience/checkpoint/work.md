Check Point was my entry point into cybersecurity—and my first and only experience inside a large corporation. I joined as part of a major effort to reinvent the company’s management experience: the interface through which administrators could see and control Check Point’s broad portfolio of security products.

For years, new products and capabilities had been added without reconsidering the experience as a whole. Each addition may have made sense individually, but together they created a management interface with more than twenty tabs layered across the screen. Users had to understand how the organization had built the product before they could understand how to operate it.

The new experience was conceived from the ground up. We first rethought the information architecture and the relationships between products, objects, policies, and daily security tasks. That foundation was followed by an entirely modern interface designed to make an increasingly powerful platform feel like one coherent system.

The effort took almost two years and involved hundreds of developers across the organization. At that scale, redesigning the product also meant navigating its internal structure: different teams, technical constraints, ownership boundaries, release cycles, and competing priorities. I learned that solving the UX was only part of the work—the design also had to survive the organization required to build it.

I was embedded with an R&D group, translating deeply technical requirements into user flows, interactive prototypes, and production-ready behavior. This was before browser-based SaaS became the default, so much of my craft was developed through complex desktop applications. The scale of Check Point also gave me unusual variety: alongside the main management platform, I moved between web experiences and several mobile projects, often encountering a completely different product problem every few weeks or months.

Check Point taught me cybersecurity, sharpened my ability to untangle complex systems, and showed me how products evolve when hundreds of people contribute to them over many years. It also taught me how design decisions move through a corporation—including the politics surrounding them.

By the time I left, I had learned how to redesign at enterprise scale. I was ready to take those lessons somewhere smaller, where I could help shape the product from its beginning.
